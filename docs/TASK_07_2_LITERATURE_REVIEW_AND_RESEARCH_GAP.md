## 1. Review method and limits

I reviewed the six research papers [1–6] in their full publisher/proceedings or arXiv PDFs, using the latest source versions available on 24 November 2026. I checked bibliographic metadata against USENIX, arXiv, IJCAI, ACL Anthology, and official project/documentation pages where available. The OWASP WSTG, OWASP Benchmark, Juice Shop, LangGraph, and Chroma pages were inspected as project documentation. The ZAP Active Scan page could not be retrieved by the web research tool during this task; the prior TASK 07.1 audit recorded that it had checked the official page, but it is not relied on for a substantive claim here.

This is a focused critical review of a preselected candidate list, not a systematic literature review. The list is not a complete map of web security testing, HTTP security-header research, AI evidence-grounding, prompt injection, uncertainty estimation, or authorization systems. The gap below is limited to these reviewed sources and must not be stated as proof that no other research exists.

## 2. Thematic review

### 2.1 Traditional web-security testing and detector benchmarks

The OWASP Web Security Testing Guide (WSTG) is a practitioner testing guide, not an empirical model evaluation. Its versioned 4.2 release is dated 3 December 2020 [7]. The separate live WSTG project page is a changing project document [8]. These sources are useful for situating security assessment as a planned testing activity, but they do not establish that AEGISCORE's five header rules are complete, correct for every deployment, or equivalent to a full web-application assessment. AEGISCORE's passive HTTP HEAD observation is intentionally much narrower.

OWASP Benchmark describes a Java and Python test suite for evaluating vulnerability-detection tools [9]. It is relevant as an example of a detector benchmark, but AEGISCORE did not run that suite: its fixture experiment tests repeatability and evidence-link membership for one constructed header observation, not detector accuracy on labeled vulnerabilities. OWASP Juice Shop is a deliberately insecure training application [10], but it was not the source of the AEGISCORE fixture and was not assessed in this thesis evaluation. The official ZAP Active Scan documentation was not retrievable in this audit, so no comparison with ZAP is made. In any event, no ZAP baseline was run.

The candidate list contains no peer-reviewed study specifically validating AEGISCORE's chosen header rules, their false-positive rates, or their relationship to application risk. That is a limitation of the source set and the prototype evaluation, not evidence that such work does not exist.

### 2.2 LLM-assisted penetration testing

**PentestGPT [1].** Deng et al. address the difficulty of automating expert penetration-testing work and the lack of evaluations that measure progress through the testing process. They construct a benchmark from 13 training targets, decomposed into 182 subtasks across 26 categories and 18 CWE items. Three certified penetration testers independently work targets and prepare walkthroughs for validation. Their exploratory evaluation compares GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Bard with the benchmark; the PentestGPT system adds Reasoning, Generation, and Parsing modules around a task-tree and feedback-loop workflow. A human tester executes the proposed actions and returns observations, so the workflow is interactive rather than fully autonomous. The paper reports stronger results on easier targets and continuing difficulty on harder targets; it also analyzes hallucinated or unnecessary operations. The authors discuss possible benchmark contamination and reproducibility concerns. Although they attempt to reduce contamination risk by selecting recent machines, that does not eliminate all benchmark or model-version threats. Its relevance is that it demonstrates a much broader, multi-step LLM penetration-testing design and uses subtask progress as evidence. It does not validate AEGISCORE's passive detector, nor does it test an evidence-reference-only advisory layer.

**Towards Automated Penetration Testing [2].** Isozaki et al. seek a reproducible task benchmark for comparing LLM penetration-testing capability, building on PentestGPT's benchmark approach. Their benchmark uses 13 VulnHub machines and 152 tasks (the original paper's Table 1 sums to 152; Happe and Cito's later review lists 162, so the primary paper's table is used here). Tasks are categorized and have walkthrough-derived expected steps. They evaluate GPT-4o and Llama 3.1-405B through PentestGPT and conduct ablations including structured task-list generation and context summaries to address planning hallucination and forgetting. The paper reports that the models do not reliably complete end-to-end tests and that performance varies across reconnaissance, exploitation, and privilege-escalation tasks. The authors identify human participation and incomplete walkthrough coverage as limitations: testers can introduce variation, and the documented paths may not include every valid solution. The paper reports only one trial per task and runs ablations on two boxes, further limiting estimates of stochastic variation and breadth. This work reinforces that task-level benchmark outcomes are not interchangeable with a narrow deterministic configuration observation. It supplies no live-provider or calibration evidence for AEGISCORE.

### 2.3 Evaluation design and evidence quality

**Benchmarking Practices in LLM-driven Offensive Security [3].** Happe and Cito review 19 research papers describing 18 prototypes and their testbeds. Rather than proposing a new pentest agent or running a common benchmark across the systems, they compare testbed provenance, scope, task structure, model selection, sample size, stop conditions, metrics, and qualitative analysis. They report substantial variation in testbed design and recommend clearer provenance, baselines, subtask success criteria, larger and better-explained samples, quantitative metrics, and qualitative trace analysis. They also caution that CTF-based environments do not necessarily represent real-world penetration testing and that benchmark choices constrain conclusions. The paper's declared scope is offensive LLM use, not testing the security of LLMs themselves. This is directly relevant to how the AEGISCORE fixture result must be described: a single repeated synthetic fixture supports a deterministic consistency claim, not real-world generalization or accuracy.

**PentestEval [4].** Yang et al. decompose penetration testing into six stages: information collection, weakness gathering, weakness filtering, attack decision-making, exploit generation, and exploit revision. They build 346 tasks across 12 vulnerable scenarios. Two certified penetration testers design scenarios and three more experts annotate/cross-check ground truth. The study evaluates nine LLMs and multiple existing tools using more than 3,000 stage evaluations and 180 end-to-end tests. The paper reports weak average performance in difficult stages such as attack decision-making and exploit generation; its end-to-end results vary substantially across the evaluated systems and conditions. It also tests controlled interventions such as ground-truth stage inputs. The stated scope is mainly web-facing and external-network testing, and the authors acknowledge that even five experts may miss valid attack paths. The results are benchmark-specific; they are not a direct baseline for AEGISCORE, whose detector does not perform exploit generation or vulnerability discovery across attack stages.

### 2.4 Multi-agent security assessment

**PENTESTLLMAGENT [5].** Sheng et al. study autonomous IP-to-root testing and introduce AutoPentest-Bench, with 13 VulnHub targets and 93 subtasks. They first analyze 130 interaction logs from 10 LLM configurations and identify rigid planning, contextual forgetting, and command-generation errors. Their framework combines a task-dependency graph for planning/backtracking, a hierarchical multi-agent architecture, and retrieval of prevalidated executable commands. On their benchmark, the best reported configuration achieves a 77% end-to-end success rate and 88% subtask completion rate; the paper reports these as results for that system, benchmark, and set of targets. Those figures are not comparable to AEGISCORE's fixture-consistency metric. The controlled VulnHub benchmark and IP-to-root objective are also materially different from AEGISCORE's exact-endpoint, one-request passive scope. This paper supports discussion of multi-agent research but does not justify adding agents to AEGISCORE or describing its separate FSM as a multi-agent pentest system.

### 2.5 Uncertainty, abstention, and advisory output

**Uncertainty Quantification in LLM Agents [6].** Oh et al. formulate uncertainty over interactive agent trajectories rather than only single-turn answers. They analyze estimator trade-offs and challenges involving heterogeneous sources, uncertainty dynamics, and a lack of fine-grained evaluation. Their numerical study uses the τ²-bench agent benchmark with GPT-4.1 and Kimi-K2.5; the paper evaluates trajectory-level uncertainty against task outcome because the benchmark does not provide turn-level labels, using metrics including AUROC and rank correlations. The work is a conceptual foundation plus numerical analysis, not a calibrated reviewer for security-header claims. Its relevance is methodological: an application's “abstained” status is not evidence of calibrated uncertainty. AEGISCORE's local reviewer tests only schema/reference/claim constraints with deterministic fake responses; it has no provider-generated evaluation data or calibration result.

### 2.6 Evidence grounding and authorization boundaries

The reviewed set offers adjacent mechanisms—PentestGPT's Parsing module, expert walkthroughs, and PentestEval's expert-annotated stage ground truth—but none directly evaluates the semantic entailment of an advisory statement from a specific HTTP evidence item, or the security of binding an external authorization record to one canonical target endpoint. The ACL paper [6] treats uncertainty in interactive agents but does not establish security-claim grounding. The WSTG [7, 8] is a practitioner guide, not a cryptographic authorization-provenance design. The candidate list therefore does not support a literature-based claim of novelty in these areas.

Prompt injection is similarly under-covered: no candidate directly studies malicious instructions embedded in HTTP response headers or assessment evidence. AEGISCORE's implementation and tests treat evidence as untrusted and constrain the advisory output, but those are project controls and test results, not proof of comprehensive prompt-injection resistance.

## 4. Research-gap argument and contribution boundary

Within the six reviewed research papers [1–6], the dominant empirical setting is broad, active, multi-stage penetration testing on vulnerable training targets or benchmark scenarios. The sources emphasize task planning, exploit-capable automation, model capability, benchmark construction, or agent uncertainty. The OWASP documents [7–10] provide practice and test resources, but they do not evaluate the same combined system boundary as AEGISCORE.

The bounded difference that motivates this thesis is the integration target: a numeric-loopback-only and endpoint-bound passive HTTP observation; a single `HEAD` request with no redirect following or body inspection; deterministic header/configuration findings linked to persisted evidence; optional advisory commentary that cannot create official findings or change severity/status; and lease-validated persistence/recovery. **No paper in this 6-paper candidate subset was found to evaluate that exact combination.** This statement describes the reviewed subset, not the entire field, and does not establish algorithmic novelty or superiority.

The defensible contribution is an implemented, tested prototype integration and a small reproducible fixture evaluation of deterministic output and evidence-link invariants. It is not a new scanning algorithm, an autonomous penetration tester, a validated security-header accuracy model, a calibrated AI reviewer, a production authorization service, or an OS-isolated deployment. The custom FSM subsystem exists elsewhere in the repository but is not the orchestrator for this passive assessment route.

### Implemented capability checks against the reviewed research context

- The API route dispatches via `AuthorizedAssessmentExecutor`; it does not call the HTTP engine directly. The executor checks persisted target binding and authorization, creates a guarded policy/transport boundary, and performs the exact authorized dispatch (`aegiscore/api/routes.py`, `aegiscore/production_execution.py`).
- Target configuration accepts explicit numeric loopback registrations and endpoint scheme/host/port/path bindings (`aegiscore/target_authorization.py`). The execution code performs one `HEAD`, bounds the timeout, disables redirect following and proxy use, captures allowlisted headers only, and verifies the connected peer (`aegiscore/execution.py`, `aegiscore/production_execution.py`).
- `PassiveHeaderDetector` emits up to five configuration observations for missing CSP, MIME-sniffing protection, framing protection, Referrer Policy, and (for HTTPS) HSTS. Findings carry deterministic IDs and evidence references (`aegiscore/passive_detector.py`). These are header observations, not confirmation that a vulnerability is exploitable.
- The reviewer is opt-in and separate; strict output/reference checks and failure isolation are implemented in `aegiscore/assessment_ai.py` and `aegiscore/api/routes.py`. Live model-provider quality has not been evaluated, and the default reviewer is disabled.
- SQLite persistence writes evidence, findings, execution result, and terminal status transactionally with lease validation. Recovery handles stale work (`aegiscore/local_persistence.py`). The standard application factory has a fail-closed verifier injection contract, but no production authority verifier is configured; no genuine approval was created or used (`aegiscore/api/app.py`).
- The numeric-loopback application controls and test fixtures do not demonstrate operating-system egress isolation. Debian 13 verification remains deferred under TASK 06.4.4.

## 5. Experimental evidence and interpretation

The existing TASK 06.9 fixture report records 100 calls against one synthetic HTTPS response, one normalized complete finding set, five findings per call, and 500/500 finding-to-evidence ID links. This is a repeatability and referential-membership result for that fixed fixture. It is not detection accuracy, a sample of 100 targets, or an estimate of field performance. The report's advisory tests use deterministic fake-adapter responses; they establish represented validator and failure-isolation behavior, not live-model quality. See [TASK 06.9 evaluation](TASK_06_9_THESIS_EVALUATION.md).

The TASK 06.9 report also records 863 Python tests passing at that historical run. The prior TASK 07.1 audit separately records a rerun of the full Python suite with 863 passed; neither count is a test rerun performed for this literature review. Historical Flutter results and current runtime limitations remain as stated in TASK 07.1; they are not AI reviewer evaluation results. No live model evaluation, human-adjudicated header dataset, false-positive/false-negative estimate, benchmark comparison, or operating-system isolation result is available.
